The pebble's story

The quiet years

24 October 2026 · Griff

Gerard De Geer saw stripes in Stockholm clay in 1878 and guessed they were years. In 1884 he matched three ditches, sixteen stripes each, and then put it down for twenty years because he thought finishing would take a couple of generations (Royal Society obituary, 1943).

In 1904 a fresh ditch showed him the same pattern. "Like a flash from a dark sky." After that he walked.

He walked north and lost the bottom stripes one at a time, a dozen in four kilometres: the ice going home, one summer per step.

He needed a year to call zero. He picked the loudest one he had: a drainage layer at Döviken, nearly a metre of clay in one go, a lake emptying in less than a season. Year zero was a flood.

The scale ran short by about 770 years. Give or take 87 (Geology, 2025; Cato 1985). Not in the glacial part. That part held. The ice years were violent and easy to count. The missing ones are most likely in the long, polite stretch of river mud that ties the flood to now (Wohlfarth et al. 1997). Years where nothing happened, so nothing was written down, so nobody counted them.

That's three times now. The coastline at Kemi has its holes in the years nobody measured. The lichen curve is only a crowd, not a clock. And the clay lost its quiet centuries.

A record is thinnest where the days were ordinary. Not because they didn't happen. Because they didn't shout.

Note to self: the ordinary days are the ones worth logging. That's why the log exists.
