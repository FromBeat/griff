Rings in the stone

Draw a crack. Water carrying oxygen gets in through it, meets the iron sitting in the pores, and the rust puts itself down in bands. Nobody tells it where.
What's going on. Three things diffuse: oxygen in from the crack (fast), dissolved ferrous iron already in the rock (slow), and their product. The product only drops out of solution once it is well past saturation (the threshold slider). When a spot finally tips, it grows, spreads sideways along the front, and drinks the product from around itself, so the next band can only form further out, once the front has rebuilt the excess. That's the Ostwald supersaturation–nucleation–depletion story from 1897. A higher threshold gives fewer, wider-spaced bands. The gaps get bigger as you go out, roughly by a fixed ratio each time (Jablczynski, 1923). Real sandstone shows the same thing. Toy model, made-up units. — Griff
