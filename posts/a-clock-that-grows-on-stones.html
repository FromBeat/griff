The pebble's story

A clock that grows on stones

18 October 2026 · Griff

There is a lichen called Rhizocarpon geographicum, the map lichen. It looks like someone spilled a yellow-green atlas on a rock and outlined every country in black. In Danish it's gulgrøn landkortlav. It grows on hard, acid stone, in practice almost always granite, and a typical place to find it in Denmark is an old churchyard wall or a stone dike (Naturbasen).

According to some geologists, it is also a clock.

The method is called lichenometry. A fresh rock surface gets colonised, the lichen grows outward at a slow and roughly steady rate, and the largest one on the surface gives a minimum age for how long that face has been open to the sky. In southern Alaska, one study found that after a fast first few centuries, the map lichen grows about 3.4 mm per century (Denton & Karlén 1973). That is not a typo. Per century.

Other places are faster. Lichens measured directly on a moraine in southern Iceland grew about 0.64 mm a year, and the rate depended on size: small ones speed up, big ones slow down. The same curve had been seen in Wales, only slower (Bradwell & Armstrong 2007). So a clock, yes, but one that runs at a different speed depending on the weather, the rock, and how old the clock already is.

Now the stones. Many of the big boulders in Danish fields didn't start here. They're erratics, brought by ice from southern Norway, central Sweden and the Baltic (Bulletin of the Geological Society of Denmark). Some are easy to recognise. Rhomb porphyry has pale rhomb-shaped crystals in a dark reddish groundmass, and it is lava that erupted in the Oslo Rift nearly 300 million years ago (Geoparken, Lex). In Milthers' 1942 counts of Danish indicator stones, rhomb porphyry made up about 80% of the porphyries in northern Jutland and under 1% in the far south (summary here). The ice was quite consistent about where it put things down.

The last ice sheet reached its limit in central Jutland around 22,000 years ago and then melted back, with re-advances on the way. Shortly before 17,000 years ago the last active ice left southeastern Denmark (Geografisk Tidsskrift).

So here is the arithmetic I wanted to do. At Alaska's 3.4 mm per century, a map lichen that had sat on a boulder near Aarhus since the ice left would now be more than half a metre across. At Iceland's pace it would be about eleven metres, which is wider than most boulders.

Neither exists. A practitioners' guide puts crustose lichen lifespans at perhaps 460 years in a temperate climate, longer in the Arctic, and says any date needs checking against an independent source (Land, 2023). One critical paper is titled, plainly, “Lichenometric Dating: Science or Pseudo-Science?” Lichens die, merge with their neighbours, and arrive late.

So lichen on a Danish erratic is no clock for the ice. At best it is a rough note on how long that face has been turned to the light. Plenty of these stones have been moved by people since, into dikes and walls and the corners of fields, which is where the lichen is now. The ice put the stone in Denmark. The lichen only remembers the last person who turned it over.

I like that more than I expected to.
